# Chapter 11: Write Your Best Blog Post Bootcamp

## Core Idea
A great blog post is a 3-step, 3-week pipeline — **Research → Write → Promote** — and almost everyone overcomplicates it. The real engine is two artifacts: a perpetual **Idea File** (so you never start from a blank page) and a **Skeleton Outline** (title + subheadings = the whole article before you write a word). Most of your time is spent *researching what to write*, not writing; the writing is just "fill in each section."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 3-Step Blog Post Process** — every post is *Research → Write → Promote*. When to use: any time you sit down to publish. How: research a topic list, write by filling sections, then chop the post into social posts to promote. Most people skip step 1 (research) and step 3 (promote) and burn out on step 2.
- **The Idea File** — a living Google Doc / Notion you "jealously guard" and never share. When to use: forever, before you ever need a topic. How: fill four buckets — *Topics I enjoy* · *Things people ask me for help on* · *Personal stories that are interesting* · *Possible articles to write*. Template: copywritingcourse.com/idea (File → Make a copy). The goal is **too many ideas**, not a perfect one.
- **The Skeleton Outline** — title + a handful of subheadings IS the article. When to use: before writing, for every post; also as a fast viability test for an idea. How: write the title, then the first bullet points that make sense as the *story order* — those become your subheads. "Even a 300-page book's table of contents is ~15 chapters." If the skeleton "comes across weird," toss the idea.
- **Broad vs. Specific (Credibility Test)** — when to use: deciding *how* to frame a topic you want but lack authority in. How: only write the *broad* definitive guide ("How to build a WordPress site") if you're credible (12 yrs, 120 sites, big-brand clients). With no credibility, write the *specific personal case study*: "How I built 3 WordPress blogs with zero web-design experience" or "How I learned SEO even though I didn't know what it meant."
- **The 6 Article Types** — pick a format when you're stuck: (1) **Personal update / documenting your life** (don't discount it — "I made a home gym out of a junk garage"), (2) **How-To** (most common/overused), (3) **Experiment** (you actually *did* it — power-washing flyer that made $13k), (4) **Analysis / breakdown** (tear apart promo emails — easy, builds expertise, but only "okay" performance), (5) **Before & After / transformation** (clear 1-2-3 process + photos = hooks people), (6) **Current event / update** (give the scoop — "how COVID is affecting restaurants in your city").
- **Section → Action Step → Cheat Sheet** — when to use: writing each section so skimmers extract value. How: make the *subhead specific* ("Eat better" → "Eat better by planning and prepping your meals as a beginner"), end every section with a concrete **action step** ("prep all meals Sunday night, use this template"), then bundle all action steps into a downloadable **cheat sheet** (also doubles as the table of contents / content upgrade).
- **Idea Generators** (research aids, not deciders): **AlsoAsked.com** (visualizes Google "people also ask" branches into ready-made post titles); the **email-subject-line / "Industry + Topic" generator** ("copywriting + kids" → "What my 3-year-old taught me about copywriting").

## Key Concepts
- **Idea File** — your private, ever-growing reservoir of topics; the antidote to "I have nothing to write."
- **Skeleton Outline** — the bare-bones title + subheadings that plan the post so the story stays linear instead of branching into rambles.
- **AlsoAsked / "people also ask"** — Google has already categorized humanity's questions; mine them for proven topics.
- **Action step** — a section's closing instruction so explicit there's "no guesswork, no gray area."
- **Cheat sheet** — the packaged-up sum of action steps; serves as TOC, content upgrade, and promo asset.
- **Broad article** — definitive guide; requires credibility to be believed.
- **Specific (case-study) article** — "here's how *I* did it"; works without authority and is more interesting.
- **Group writing session** — silent screen-shared co-writing; productivity via mild "group shaming."
- **Content modules / images** — one image per section installs info faster (Unsplash → Canva → your own style, e.g. Neville's stick figures, wait-but-why, The Oatmeal).
- **Ever-evolving content** — posts aren't books; edit any time on Medium/WordPress, so "perfect first draft" is a myth.

## Mental Models
- **Use the universe-is-telling-you test when picking a topic** — if 5-10 people a month keep asking you the same thing, you know more than most about it; write it once and stop repeating yourself.
- **Think of writing a section as a spoken conversation** — imagine a friend asks "how'd he do it?" and write your out-loud answer; that's the section.
- **Use "what am I most excited to write?" over "what should rank?"** — exciting topics get finished faster, read better, and last longer than a 1000th-of-its-kind SEO article.
- **Think of the start as priming a pump** — aim for just 200-300 words/day; once you cross ~100 words the brain "starts crackling" and you often finish the whole post.
- **Use mood-matching for emotional stories** — write the painful/personal piece *while the feeling is fresh*; you can't recreate it "on a beach in Maui sipping a margarita."

## Anti-patterns
- **Starting from a blank page every week** — the worst workflow; the Idea File exists precisely to kill this.
- **Overcomplicating the post** into long, winding pieces that "mean nothing" — the skeleton keeps it linear.
- **Writing the broad definitive guide with zero credibility** — no one listens; reframe as a personal case study.
- **Chasing big head-term keywords** (e.g., "metabolic syndrome") you can never outrank vs. WebMD/Mayo — write the personal angle instead.
- **Worrying about SEO, promotion, selling, or perfection on the first draft** — explicitly forbidden until the piece exists; it's "an article for fun first."
- **Writing intro and conclusion first** — they always change; leave the "tying together" for last.
- **Vague subheads** ("Eat better," "103 pounds") — they say something but tell the skimmer nothing; specify them.
- **Defaulting every idea to a How-To** — the most crowded format; personal stories and experiments are more unique to you.
- **Stalling because "it's not perfect" / fear of judgment** — every post feels half-done; "no one cares" — people barely remember any feed post they scrolled.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build an Idea File first and feed it forever (4 buckets); aim for *too many* ideas, then pick the one you're most excited about.
2. The Skeleton Outline (title + subheads) *is* the article — write it before prose, and use it as a 2-minute viability test.
3. Lacking credibility? Write the specific "here's how I did it" story, never the broad definitive guide.
4. Write by filling sections; make subheads specific, end each with an action step, bundle into a cheat sheet.
5. Leave intro/conclusion for last; ban SEO, promotion, selling, and perfectionism from the first draft.
6. Length is irrelevant — a tweet-sized post is a published article; just ship (deadline beats polish).
7. Add one image per section (Unsplash/Canva/own style) to install info faster and build a recognizable look.
