# Chapter 9: Becoming a Copywriter & Getting Clients

## Core Idea
Don't study your way into a copywriting career — **write your way in, then post an offer and ask for money**. The skill is necessary but the bottleneck is action: pick something you can already do or have credibility in, post a tiny offer to people who already trust you, charge a minimum of $100, and let real demand (not theory) tell you what to specialize in. "The number one way to tell what you want to do is by seeing what you don't want to do."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The $100 Goal** — Aim for exactly your first $100, not a business. When to use: any beginner frozen by fear or overthinking. How: post one concrete service offer and collect $100 into your account. Purpose is psychological — once someone experiences "I offered something, they paid me, I keep the money," their brain permanently changes and they repeat it. The number is small on purpose so the fear (not the money) is the real obstacle being removed.
- **Cheap, Fast, Dirty** — The class motto. When to use: setting up to land a first gig. How: no fancy web pages, no Facebook ads, no money spent, no design polish. Most first sales happen within 10 minutes of posting. Do not "courseify" or incorporate an LLC — just make a Google Doc and present.
- **The Lazy Google Doc Portfolio** — When to use: always; build it before pitching anything. How: `File → Make a copy`, list **Technical Skills** (every software you touch), **Real-World Skills** (welding, driving, bowling, buying a home), **Previous Experience** (anything you were ever paid for), optional testimonials, and contact info at the bottom. "No one cares how your portfolio looks." Share-by-link, instant updates, no HTML/hosting/DNS. The act of filling it out reveals skills people forgot they had.
- **The Friends-&-Family-First Posting Order** — When to use: the very first post. How: post the offer to your personal Facebook (or LinkedIn/Twitter) before Craigslist/groups, because credibility is already there. Friends who don't need the service tag people who do. Then **broaden** to alternative places (Craigslist in the US, Gumtree in EU/AU, Thumbtack/TaskRabbit for physical services). Give it 24–48 hours to marinate.
- **Three-Pronged Pricing (Basic / Advanced / Expensive)** — When to use: clients want more than the $100 covers. How: $100 = bare-bones basic with explicitly *restricted* scope ("for $100 you get this, this, this"); a mid package (~$500) for more; an open-ended top tier ($2,000–$3,000+ or hourly). Pitch the $100, upsell the rest. "We charge a minimum of $100, but there's no maximum."
- **Double the Price** — When to use: an offer gets a flood of leads fast (e.g. 20 leads in an hour). How: don't take the post down — double the price; if it still floods, double again. This is exactly how freelancers and agencies scale: get clients, then keep raising rates.
- **The Become-a-Copywriter Ladder** — Are you writing? → Get exposure (be active in small Facebook groups/forums for ~3 months) → Get good (immerse in the craft) → Offer free help/critiques → Document everything → Network *outside* copywriting rooms. Sequence for someone with no clients yet.

## Key Concepts
- **Get the ball rolling** — early effort is pushing a heavy ball; expect to work hard (sometimes free) for the first months before momentum.
- **Document, document, document** — capture before/after screenshots and links of every job; proof of transformation ("0.001% → 5%") sells far better than a finished artifact alone.
- **Offer free help** — for true beginners with no following: "send me a topic and I'll write you an article / critique your page free," then log it in the portfolio.
- **Restrict the scope** — vague offers attract clients wanting way more than $100; spell out exactly what's included and charge extra beyond it.
- **The phone call beats the email** — cold email is only a sentiment probe; the person with the "gumption" to call directly gets hired. Best combo: send a short email, then call as follow-up.
- **Ultra-short cold copy** — one-to-two-sentence emails land in the primary inbox and convert best; long, joke-laden, over-thought copy does worse. "The shortest, most non-thought-out email tends to work best almost every single time."
- **Plain English ≠ wacky** — casual/conversational is fine ("we care about our customers"); plain language gets the point across without being a goofy jackass.
- **Buzzwords are unfinished thinking** — if a word ("efficient," "productive," "sociocratic") can be broken down further, it's a buzzword; replace it with a concrete before/after comparison and back-of-envelope ROI numbers.

## Mental Models
- **Let the universe (the market) niche you down** — go *broad* when beginning, go *niche* after you have success. Don't pre-pick "dentist about-pages"; the kinds of clients who keep hiring you, and the content people keep sharing, will reveal your specialty for you (Neville → software clients, then "copywriter," via repeated signal).
- **Think of the first gig as the cheapest experiment you can run** — if no one in your circle will pay even $100, that's not failure, it's a fast, free signal to pivot. You spent no money and barely any time.
- **Hire (and be hired) on "how much have they written," not credentials** — the buyer's lens: ignore the English degree/PhD, look at whether they write constantly and have a domain skill (engineering, law, teaching). People who like writing just write — and are likelier to be good.
- **Future-proof by being good + adjacent-skilled, not cheapest** — you can't win on price against cheaper global labor; win on quality, reliability, communication, fitting the team, and stacking ~80%-proficiency skills (a "triple threat") rather than one commodity skill. Stay on top of new tech instead of fearing it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting until you "feel ready"** — fear of being judged for selling is the #1 thing that stops first money; people forget your post in seconds, and if nobody buys, nothing bad happens.
- **Charging too little "because I'm new"** — $20 for a resume can never become a real income; the answer is always $100+. Underpricing also signals you don't value the work.
- **Building a fancy portfolio website** — you become a crappy web designer instead of a writer; almost nobody cold-buys off a portfolio site. Use the Google Doc.
- **Niching down too early** — kills your addressable market before you know your edge (the podcaster-only email tool that had to retreat to "general").
- **"Courseifying" before you've freelanced** — turning a skill into a course first = hundreds of hours, little money; just sell the service ("I'll make the bot for you") and courseify later if it works.
- **Doing big free "spec" proposals** — companies harvest your plan (AT&T-style) and pick no one; send warm-but-serious leads to a paid consult instead, crediting it toward the project.
- **Hiding your best credentials out of modesty** — the VP-of-18-years, the PhD, the "sold deals with Dell/EMC" — flaunt them; they make people take you seriously instantly.
- **Stacking jokes / over-writing to "be funny"** — paragraphs of setup for a weak punchline buries the offer ("wait, what are you selling?"); one word ("giggity") can carry the joke. Max one joke per few paragraphs.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Write first, theorize never** — if you haven't written anything, you're at a disadvantage; start a free Blogger/Medium blog today and publish regularly. This also reveals whether you even like writing.
2. **Build the lazy Google Doc portfolio before you pitch** — listing technical + real-world + paid experience surfaces sellable skills you forgot you had.
3. **Post a concrete $100 offer to friends & family first**, then broaden to Craigslist/Gumtree; give it 24–48 hours.
4. **Restrict scope, price at $100 minimum, and double when flooded** — the floor is $100, there is no ceiling.
5. **Get on the phone** — cold email is just a probe; calling directly is the highest-leverage, lowest-downside move.
6. **Keep cold copy ultra-short and plain** — one or two sentences; conversational, not wacky; cut buzzwords for before/after specifics and ROI math.
7. **Stay broad, document everything, and let the market niche you** — specialization emerges from the work people actually pay for and share.
