# Chapter 5: Sales Pages, eCommerce & Page Reviews

## Core Idea
A sales page is **anywhere you try to sell on a single page** — and the right length is dictated by how much the buyer already knows. Match effort to the decision: a known product needs only a buy button; an unfamiliar or considered purchase needs the full **AIDA** sequence (plus testimonials) so you trigger every psychological checkpoint before asking for the sale. For eCommerce, the page's first job isn't persuasion — it's to **replicate you on a showroom floor and answer every question the customer would otherwise phone or email about.**

## Frameworks Introduced
- **AIDA (Attention · Interest · Desire · Action)** — the same psychological formula from the email chapter, reused as the spine of every sales page. **When to use:** any page selling a considered/unfamiliar product. **How:** Attention = headline + sub-headline (give the entire gist) + a picture under the headline; Interest = it's easier than they think / what it does; Desire = what new world this opens for them; Action = the explicit schedule/price/where-when-what, "pay here today," plus a P.S. of light scarcity ("spots filling up quick").
- **Modified AIDA = "AIDA + T"** — AIDA with **Testimonials** inserted (and optionally a **benefits comparison**). **When to use:** as the default sales-page skeleton. **How:** Headline(s) → Interest → Desire → **Testimonial** → image/product shot → product comparison → exact how-to-buy → Action. Drop testimonials anywhere; that's the only structural difference from plain AIDA.
- **The Sales Page Outline Template** — a fill-in doc so a long page "builds itself." **When to use:** when a blank sales page feels intimidating. **How:** write each short section separately (multiple headlines, Interest, Desire, a testimonial, an image, a comparison, the buy step, Action), then stack them verbatim — the assembled stack reads as one smooth sales letter. A giant sales letter is just many short sections glued together.
- **The Amazon Product-Page Formula (steal it)** — pull up the most-trafficked store on earth (e.g. a Dyson page) and copy what they show on *every* product. **When to use:** building any eCommerce listing. **How:** name · price · reviews · stock-left · one-line summary → full description (what it is, what it does, special features, why it's better) → then the "boring" mandatory block: dimensions, weight, batteries/power, what's in the box, shipping weight/speed, complementary products. Skip only fields you genuinely can't fill (e.g. delivery date).

## Key Concepts
- **Match length to buyer knowledge** — a Googled, branded product needs almost no copy ("add to cart"); a digital/training/unfamiliar product needs you to explain why, what it does, what's included, what happens after purchase, and how to buy.
- **Your store = you, replicated** — write it to answer the showroom-floor questions ("what batteries?" "what's it work with?"). 80% of one company's phone calls were a single unanswered spec.
- **Sometimes copy isn't the best thing** — for highly visual products (a shirt, a dog) a picture or video sells better than prose; only add text for the part that *isn't* visually represented.
- **Each product page IS a landing page** — never offload differentiators to "another page." If it doesn't cost anything to add to the page, add it; mobile buyers won't hunt across pages.
- **Collective testimonial effect** — people rarely read individual testimonials; they scan the *mass* ("he likes it, she likes it") and conclude "people like it." Volume signals trust more than any single quote.
- **The reason-why purchase** — buyers infer "$500 must mean it's good"; price itself is a claim of quality.
- **Sciencey believability** — a credible mechanism (emu-oil prism story, plant stem cells) converts to sales because it gives the buyer a *reason* the product works.
- **Most products are technically similar, separated by marketing** — Gary Halbert / Tova Borgnine perfume: the best-selling scent wrapped in story. Real differentiation (genuine novel ingredient) is worth surfacing; manufactured difference lives in positioning.
- **"The selling is done beforehand"** — for strong brands the page barely matters (iPhone could say "buy"); fix the email sequence / pre-frame before obsessing over the sales page.

## Mental Models
- **Use AIDA as scaffolding, not a script** — when a long page paralyzes you, fill short blocks per letter and stack them; the formula does the psychological ordering for you.
- **Think of an eCommerce page as a showroom employee** — if a question would be asked on the floor or the phone, it belongs on the page; every unanswered question is a sale leaking to a competitor.
- **Work backwards from the end goal** — review a page by asking "what's the one action?" (get them on the phone / apply / buy), confirm that CTA appears in nearly every section and at the bottom, *then* judge the copy.
- **Treat the page as a fancy business card** — for a personal-brand/coaching site, it exists to get the prospect to the next step, not to say everything.
- **Story over spec, ending in a punch** — show the product/history/proof to build interest, then "bam" — ask for the order at the very end; never bury the checkout under more clutter below it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Over-writing a visual product** — paragraphs about a shirt's softness when a worn-on photo sells it; wasted effort and weaker conversion.
- **Splitting differentiators across pages** — "the other page explains that." Scattershot facts the buyer must click around to assemble = they never assemble them, especially on mobile.
- **Putting the checkout mid-page with junk below it** — kills the close; move the buy block to the bottom after the story.
- **Being clever with labels** — "AUA" instead of "Contact," cute acronyms, pun CTAs ("free to move about"). People don't get the joke; clever "never works well." Use plain English.
- **Buzzword/flowery copy** — "innovation personified," "cultural competence and awareness." For small-client and non-enterprise audiences, plainer and more concise always wins.
- **Harping on "we're different"** — claiming uniqueness repeatedly; the visitor is already semi-interested and doesn't believe assertions. **Show, don't claim** (case studies, measured results).
- **Carousels/sliders** — add complexity, "don't really do anything"; list items out instead.
- **Scammy "I'll reveal the secret" copy with no payoff** — the reader learns nothing and distrusts you; let them leave the copy knowing *more* than before.
- **Not capturing the phone number** when a call is the money step — adding a phone field didn't lower conversion in split tests, it nudged it up.
- **Forgetting the explicit "Order yours today" / "Apply"** — even hip brands must ask for the action plainly at the end.

## Key Takeaways
1. Right-size the page to buyer knowledge: known product → button; unfamiliar/considered → full **AIDA + Testimonials**.
2. Build long pages by stacking short AIDA-section blocks from the template — never face a blank page.
3. Steal Amazon's field list; the "boring" specs (dimensions, weight, what's in the box, compatible products) eliminate ~80% of support questions and complete the buyer's picture.
4. Treat every product page as a standalone landing page — put the differentiator, the science, and the proof right there, on mobile-friendly *text* not baked-in images.
5. Differentiate by being **better and more believable**, not weirder — surface a genuine, distilled "reason why it works" and the awards/clinical proof in plain language.
6. Review any page by working backwards from the single goal, confirming the CTA repeats and closes at the end, and stripping buzzwords, clever labels, and sliders.
7. Show over tell: photos/video for visual goods, collective testimonials for trust, varied case studies (big/medium/small client) for credibility.

## Connects To
- **AIDA & email writing** — the same AIDA formula and the "selling done beforehand" email sequence that pre-frames the sale.
- **Headlines** — sales pages start with brainstorming many headlines (write ~100, pick the best); the headline + sub-headline must convey the whole gist.
- **About-page / personal-brand writing** — face-of-the-business pages: tell your story in text, lead to the one CTA, prove yourself with case studies.
- **Positioning & differentiation** — luxury/price-driven products are won upstream in positioning, not on-page copy.
- **The "modules" idea** — menu of components (galleries, tables, maps, video) to consider per page, used as a checklist for "does this need to be better?"
