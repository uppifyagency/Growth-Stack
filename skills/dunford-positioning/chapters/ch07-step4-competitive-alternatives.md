# Chapter 7: Step 4 — List Your True Competitive Alternatives

## Core Idea
Customers' view of competitors is the only one that matters for positioning. Your differentiators are only meaningful relative to what customers would *actually* use instead of you.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The defining question**: "What would our best customers do if we didn't exist?" The answer is often *not* a named competitor — frequently "use a spreadsheet," "hire an intern," or "do nothing."
- **Ask alternatives, not problems**: customers describe their problems too narrowly/technically to reveal categories. Asking what they'd replace you *with* exposes how they categorize you (DB admins said "run a fast query," but if the DB didn't exist they'd use a BI tool or data warehouse).

## Key Concepts
- **Competitive alternative**: what target customers would use or do if your product didn't exist — their yardstick for "better."
- **You are the expert; they aren't**: you know the landscape better than customers. A competitor with tiny market share has near-zero mindshare and shouldn't be listed as a real alternative.
- **The most common B2B alternative**: a combination of general-purpose software (spreadsheets, docs, slides) plus manual processes.

## Mental Models
- "Better" is defined by the *customer's* yardstick: if their real alternative is Excel, you can't claim "easier to use" unless you're easier than a spreadsheet — not easier than a rival startup they've never heard of.
- Customers always group solutions into categories; talking about problems alone won't reveal those categories.

## Anti-patterns
- **Listing every theoretical competitor**: focus only on what your *best-fit* customers would actually consider, ranked most-common to least-common.
- **Obsessing over startups competing for investor dollars**: customers have likely never heard of them.

## Key Takeaways
1. Anchor on the best-fit customer list from Step 1; name only alternatives *they* would use.
2. Rank alternatives from most to least common; focus on the common ones.
3. Group alternatives into natural clusters (e.g. "do it manually" vs. "use a small-business accounting tool"). Teams usually land on 2–5 groups.
