# Chapter 2: Positioning as Context

## Core Idea
Customers make sense of a new product by gathering contextual clues to slot it into a frame of reference. The frame they choose can make or break the business — and you can deliberately shift it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Positioning as context-setting**: messaging, pricing, features, branding, partners, and customers together set the scene. Choose the context that makes your strengths obvious.
- **The Two Traps** (why we stick with default positioning):
  - **Trap 1**: You're stuck on what you *intended* to build and don't notice the product became something else (your "cake" is really a "cake pop").
  - **Trap 2**: You designed for a market, but the market shifted (your "diet muffin" is now a "gluten-free paleo snack").
- **Frame of reference**: most products are exceptional only inside their *best* frame. Coke ≠ fizzy water; a concert violinist ≠ a street performer.

## Key Concepts
- **The Joshua Bell experiment**: world-class violinist plays a subway station → earns $32. Even a world-class product, poorly positioned, fails. "Sabotaged by his context."
- **Default positioning**: the unconscious frame you adopt the moment you name the product ("cake," "email," "database") — it silently sets buyers, competitors, pricing, and roadmap.
- **Deliberate positioning**: consciously choosing the frame that centers your unique strengths (cake-on-a-stick is "Frankencake"; a cake pop is obviously awesome).

## Mental Models
- **"If you bake bread in a gallery, you're an artist"** (Abramović): context, not the product, decides perceived value.
- Changing the frame changes *everything*: target buyers, competitive alternatives, pricing/margin, and the product roadmap.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing there's only one obvious context for your product** — there isn't; the best position is usually not the default.
- **Letting prospects invent your position** — if they can't figure you out fast, they'll assign you a frame that hides your strengths.

## Key Takeaways
1. Context can completely transform how a product is perceived — same product, different frame, different business.
2. Products drift during development; you often end up with something other than what you set out to build (email → group chat; database → analytics).
3. Markets shift around a static product; well-positioned can become poorly-positioned without the product changing (Arm & Hammer: baking → deodorizing).
4. Repositioning changes how *you* see yourselves too — it reshapes the roadmap (database → data warehouse).
