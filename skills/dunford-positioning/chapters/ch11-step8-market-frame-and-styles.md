# Chapter 11: Step 8 — Find a Market Frame of Reference + The Three Positioning Styles

## Core Idea
Pick the market category that makes your value obvious to the segment that cares most — then choose *how* you intend to win it. This is the highest-leverage step.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ways to find the market frame**:
  - **Abductive reasoning**: "if it looks/swims/quacks like a duck…" — ask what products typically have your features and deliver your value.
  - **Examine adjacent (growing) markets**: lines blur; your value may be more at home next door. Favor fast-growing adjacents — but only if they make your strengths obvious.
  - **Ask customers (cautiously)**: they'll place you in the most obvious market linked to their job/industry, which is often *not* the best frame.
- **The Three Positioning Styles** (how to win):
  1. **Head to Head** — win an *existing* market by beating the leader at their game. Accept the current category definition and criteria. Use only if you're the leader, or the category exists with *no* clear leader. Rarely right for a small/new company ("don't out-cola Coke").
  2. **Big Fish, Small Pond** — dominate a *subsegment* with distinct unmet needs the leader ignores. You still must be "good enough" on core category criteria. Default for most startups.
  3. **Create a New Game** — create a *new category*. Sell the *problem* first; teach why the category should exist, how to evaluate it, and why you lead it. Hardest, slowest, most expensive; usually needs deep-pocketed patient investors.

## Key Concepts
- **Market category triggers assumptions** about competitors, features, and pricing — choose one that works *for* you.
- **Subsegmenting**: splitting an existing market (by industry, geography, company size, ecosystem). The subsegment must be **identifiable** (you can build a prospect list), **big enough** to hit goals, and have a **specific, important unmet need**.
- **"Why now?"**: category creation needs a strong answer — a new technology, regulation, economic shift, or change in buyer behavior.

## Mental Models
- Head to Head = "beat Coke in cola." Big Fish = "sell Coke for dogs" (a thing Coke can't copy without damaging its brand). New Game = invent the drink and the occasion.
- Category creators are forged by "freaks" — early customers extreme in attitude/behavior, who become mainstream (Eloqua's "demand generators" → "marketing automation").

## Anti-patterns
- **Picking a market just because it's hot** — only choose it if it makes your strengths obvious.
- **A small startup going Head to Head with an entrenched leader** (a "cheaper, crappier Siebel").
- **Subsegmenting a category buyers don't understand** — only adds confusion (Bic for Her: no real unmet need → ridiculed off the market).
- **Building a new category, then losing leadership** to a well-funded fast follower at the moment of mainstream understanding.

## Key Takeaways
1. Janna Systems: "enterprise CRM" (cheaper, crappier Siebel) → "CRM for investment banks" → raised prices, $2M→$70M in 18 months, acquired for $1.7B. Focusing on a niche doesn't cap growth.
2. Match style to competitive landscape + business goals; you can graduate from Big Fish → Head to Head / New Game later.
3. Category creation = selling the *problem* before the solution.
