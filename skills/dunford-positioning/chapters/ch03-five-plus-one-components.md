# Chapter 3: The Five (Plus One) Components of Effective Positioning

## Core Idea
Positioning has no standard method because no one breaks it into parts. Decompose it into five components (plus a bonus), then work them in dependency order.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Five (Plus One) Components of Effective Positioning** (work in THIS order):
  1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would use/do if you didn't exist. Often "use a spreadsheet," "hire an intern," or "do nothing" — not a named rival. This is the yardstick they use to define "better."
  2. **Unique attributes** — features/capabilities you have that the alternatives lack. Your secret sauce (tech features, delivery model, business model, expertise, partnerships).
  3. **Value (and proof)** — the benefit those attributes enable, mapped to a customer goal. Must be **provable** (data, reviewers, customer quotes) — your opinion isn't proof.
  4. **Target market characteristics** — the identifiable traits of buyers who care *a lot* about that value (buy fast, rarely discount, tell friends).
  5. **Market category** — the frame of reference that makes your value obvious and triggers powerful assumptions (competitors, features, pricing).
  6. **(Bonus) Relevant trends** — what makes you matter *right now* (e.g. "AI-empowered CRM," not just "AI").

## Key Concepts
- **The dependency flow**: alternatives → attributes → value → who cares → market category → (trends). Start anywhere else and the positioning won't click.
- **Market category triggers assumptions**: declare "CRM" and buyers assume Salesforce-like competitors, features, and per-seat pricing. A wrong category makes you fight those assumptions.

## Mental Models
- **"Why never write a positioning statement"**: the traditional FOR / IS A / WHICH PROVIDES / UNLIKE template (1) assumes you already know the answers, (2) reinforces the status quo (favors the incumbent), (3) gives no hint what to do next, (4) is unmemorable. Do the *work*, don't fill the template.
- Trends are a *characteristic* (new, noteworthy), not a category (a group of similar products). "AI" alone tells buyers nothing about what you are.

## Anti-patterns
- **Starting from your features** (comparing yourself to competitors *you* see, not the alternatives *customers* see).
- **Qualitative, unprovable value claims** ("people enjoy well-designed UIs") — customers won't believe them.
- **Layering a trend with no link to the product** — muddies the waters.

## Key Takeaways
1. Each component is meaningful only relative to the others — attributes are "unique" only versus the real alternatives.
2. The market category does the heavy lifting: pick it wisely and assumptions work *for* you.
3. Trends are optional; market category is not.
