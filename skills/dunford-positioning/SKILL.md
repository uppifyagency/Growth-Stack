## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Positioning, defined
**Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how you are the best at something that a defined market cares a lot about.** It is *context-setting* for products — the clues (messaging, pricing, features, branding, competitors) that tell customers what your product is, who it's for, and why it matters. Every marketing and sales tactic uses positioning as an input; none of the "new cool stuff" works without it. Weak positioning is a headwind on everything you do.

**Core insight: most products can be positioned in multiple markets.** The best position is rarely the default one you started with. Context can transform a product — same product, different frame of reference, entirely different business (cake → muffin → cake pop; database → data warehouse). Even a world-class product, poorly positioned, fails (the Joshua Bell subway experiment).

### The Five (Plus One) Components of Effective Positioning
Work them **in this order** — each flows from the previous:
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would use/do if you didn't exist (often a spreadsheet, an intern, or "do nothing" — not a named competitor).
2. **Unique attributes** — features/capabilities you have that the alternatives lack (your "secret sauce").
3. **Value (and proof)** — the benefit those attributes enable, mapped to a customer goal; must be provable (third-party/data, not opinion).
4. **Target market characteristics** — who cares *a lot* about that value (an actionable segment, not demographics).
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference that makes your value obvious; triggers powerful assumptions about competitors, features, pricing.
6. **(Bonus) Relevant trends** — used carefully, makes your product relevant *right now*.

### Why you should NEVER write a positioning statement
The traditional FOR/IS A/WHICH PROVIDES/UNLIKE template assumes you already know the answers, reinforces the status quo, gives no hint what to do next, and is unmemorable. Do the *work* of positioning; don't fill in a template.

### The Two Traps (signs you're stuck on default positioning)
- **Trap 1**: You're stuck on what you *intended* to build and don't realize the product became something else.
- **Trap 2**: You designed for a market, but the market moved (your "diet muffin" is now a "gluten-free paleo snack").

### The Three Positioning Styles (Step 8 — how to win your market)
- **Head to Head** — win an *existing* market by beating the leader at their own game. Rarely right for a small/new company (don't out-cola Coke). Use only if you're the leader, or the category exists but has no clear leader.
- **Big Fish, Small Pond** — dominate a *subsegment* with distinct unmet needs. The default for most startups. Subsegment must be identifiable, big enough to hit targets, with a real unmet need (Janna Systems: "CRM for investment banks" → $1.7B acquisition).
- **Create a New Game** — create a *new category*. Hardest, most expensive; sell the *problem* first. Use only when no existing category fits; usually needs deep-pocketed patient investors (Eloqua → "marketing automation").

### Feature → Benefit → Value
A feature ("15-megapixel camera") enables a benefit ("sharp images") which maps to a customer's goal/value ("zoom or print large and stay sharp"). Always push to value. **Your opinion of your strengths is irrelevant without proof.**

### The Sales Story (how to operationalize positioning)
Problem → how customers solve it today + where that falls short → "the perfect world" (ideal solution's features) → introduce product + market category → value themes (with proof) → call to action. Build this *before* messaging.

### Key rules
- Start positioning from your **best-fit customers** (the ecstatic fans), not your product.
- **Target as narrowly as you can to hit near-term sales objectives**; broaden later.
- **It's better to be a little boring than completely baffling** — only layer a trend if it has a clear link to your product and market.
- Positioning is a **business-strategy** exercise led by the business leader, done as a cross-functional team — not "owned" by marketing.
