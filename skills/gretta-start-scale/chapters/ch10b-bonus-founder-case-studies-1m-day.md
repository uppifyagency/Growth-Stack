# Chapter 10b: Bonus — Founder Case Studies & The $1M Day

## Core Idea
The bonus module's "proof" half: five founder interviews plus Gretta's flagship case study, used as a **pattern library**. The same Start & Scale lessons recur across very different products — teeth-whitening, dropshipping, swimwear, skincare, watches: build a *brand people want to be part of*, start scrappy and hands-on, diversify so no single point can sink you, turn accidents into UVPs, and treat influencer marketing as a compounding system at scale rather than a few hopeful posts.

## Founder Case Studies

### Nik Mirkovic & Alex Tomic — HiSmile (teeth whitening)
- **Found the gap on social.** They studied the market for ~a year before launching (Dec 2014). Teeth-whitening was an unsexy, undifferentiated category — people bought by default at the supermarket or dentist. No brand stood out. They built **a brand people *want* to be part of** — "be the Apple/Nike of your space" (you don't end up with Nike, you go looking for Nike).
- **Product from pain points.** Mapped every customer complaint (sensitivity, inconvenience, slow results), spec'd a product that fixed all of them, got a cosmetic chemist to approve it, then found a manufacturer who could make it simple and convenient. Launched ~3 months later.
- **Keep it in-house, master the craft.** Ran Facebook ads, influencer outreach, dispatch, and customer care internally — refused to offset core functions to agencies/"experts" who can't adapt fast (Snapchat → Stories pivots) and aren't emotionally invested in the brand vision. Hire for **work ethic + willingness to learn + positive mindset + brand belief**, not narrow expertise.
- **Influencer ROI is a sum-total, not a per-post.** Don't obsess over one post's direct sales; the *collected effort over a week/month* builds brand, and every collab yields reusable curated content to redistribute via paid ads. **Flip the outreach mindset**: ask "what can we offer *them*?" (product, payment, a share to your page). The Kylie Jenner post built brand awareness/attention, not a trackable code — and "build your foundation first" (they worked with 5-10K influencers over time): going straight to the biggest influencer with overnight-ROI expectations is why brands like Frank Body's $20K Gigi post "failed."
- **Operations.** Minimal SKUs (one product, a few variants) keeps warehousing low and enables **pre-packing** by predicted mix (single kit vs double vs kit+gel). Always operate "as if you're 6 months ahead." Founders roll up sleeves on the packing line at peak (Boxing Day). Self-funded; manage cash flow with **long supplier payment terms (90-120 days)** earned by being a high-value customer. Traction: $20M turnover → predicted $40M+; moving toward a **subscription** model for longevity.

### Nathan Hirsch — dropshipping → FreeUp (remote-talent marketplace)
- **What dropshipping is.** List products you don't stock; the supplier ships directly to the customer; you keep the margin (typically **~8-20%**). It's **cash-positive** (no upfront inventory; Amazon often pays you before you pay the vendor) but you're at the mercy of your supplier — Amazon blames *you*, not them, so vet suppliers hard and track every order to delivery.
- **Sourcing is manual sales.** Cold-email/cold-call manufacturers; pitch why they should work with you; owning your **own website** opens more doors than being "just another Amazon seller." Split manufacturers into large/medium/small and test who'll talk to you and who runs a dropship program. Expect to contact *hundreds* to find a few good ones. Start manual; automate (APIs, ShipStation, dev team) only once revenue justifies it.
- **Trial-and-error your category.** He cycled textbooks → DVDs → toys → baby/outdoor before finding baby products sold best for his system. Furniture/computers never worked. Your listing system affects which categories you can win.
- **Diversify or die.** On day one of a vacation, his sole vendor quit (2 weeks' notice) *and* his identity was stolen for a $60K tax return. The fix made him stronger: within 6 months he had many suppliers, so no single loss could end the business. **Diversify** across revenue streams (Shopify long-term + Amazon short-term), vendors, and people. Amazon = fast but you're at their mercy (suspension risk); Shopify = slower but a real, investable, owned asset.
- **Margin must cover defects.** The bigger/heavier/pricier the item, the higher the margin you need — returns and damage are inevitable in dropshipping; know your competition's margins before committing.
- **FreeUp.** Born from how long it takes to find good remote workers — a **highly vetted** marketplace ($5-$50/hr, US and non-US, every e-commerce skill) with communication-guidelines testing and a no-turnover guarantee.

### Simone Wilkins — OPV (swimwear / fashion)
- **Entered before the flood.** She and her sister picked swimwear in 2011 *because it wasn't a crowded market* — no fashion-design background; she sampled in Bali for two months, with early samples coming back terrible (e.g. size-16 errors). **Learned the hard way; stuck with it.**
- **An accident became the flagship.** A miscommunication with the manufacturer produced **two-tone bottoms** (instead of two separate colored bottoms) — they looked great, customers loved them, and they "put OPV on the map." (Echoes Gretta's "turn accidents into UVPs.")
- **Early Instagram + a single shout-out.** "3,000 followers was a lot" in the early Instagram days; a **cool shoot ≠ sales** until the brand grew. The growth inflection came when **Gretta (then Skinny Me Tea) reposted/tagged them** — and later organic wears by Kendall/Kylie Jenner and Gigi Hadid (sent free via a PR agency, *not* paid). Took ~3 years (founded 2011, took off ~2014); both founders kept day jobs until ~2015.
- **Scrappy stack, then Shopify.** Started with *no* loans, funding everything from their day jobs; first "website" was an India-bid freelancer build they edited by hand for ~a year before moving to **Shopify** (~4 years now).
- **Listen, refresh, scarcity.** A one-off **"collection" (Paradise Passport) flopped** vs their usual cadence of dropping fresh styles every few months — so they reverted. They **discontinue SKUs to keep scarcity/freshness** (retiring watermelon, hyping new avocado/peach/kiwi "fruit bottoms"); listen to customers via DMs/Stories but "draw a line." Stopped paying influencers (no clear ROI when an influencer posts 10 other labels) — now send product for content only. Customer care: reply within the hour, reassure on shipping, resend lost parcels with a freebie — most become returning customers. **Build a tribe/story people want to be part of** ("there's no such thing as an overnight success").

### Andy Hnilo — Alitura (skincare)
- **"Scratch your own itch" at the extreme.** After a near-fatal **hit-and-run** (broken jaw wired shut, 7 broken ribs, collapsed lung), he used himself as a science experiment — formulating a **nutrient-dense clay mask** (9 ingredients) plus an internal tonic (egg yolks, deer placenta, spirulina, etc.) to recover fastest. A **creative outlet became a brand only after demand appeared.**
- **Validation before "business."** **Beta-tested on friends** who'd seen his ICU recovery (visible results in ~10 days) and on a friend's med-spa clients, who loved it and added it to their treatment list — the **external validation** he needed. First "packaging" was clear grocery-store olive-oil/peanut-butter jars; first 8 orders at ~$25 each.
- **A podcast launched it.** Cold-emailed Dave Asprey (Bulletproof) with before/after photos; came on as ambassador, then a podcast appearance with millions of downloads triggered worldwide demand. Now in 75 countries, growing ~130% YoY, cash-positive (no debt).
- **Sequential product line funded by profit.** Demand pulls each new SKU (customers kept asking "what moisturizer/night cream do you use?"): mask → moisturizer → night cream → serum/scrub/supplements. **Use profit from one product to fund the next**; space releases to build buzz; survey customers for "dream products."
- **Chase excellence over margin.** Visits every manufacturer/chemist, builds from a blank canvas (not pre-made ingredient decks), insists on food-grade, glass that blocks light, bespoke sourcing (Hawaiian honey from a family with a story). Self-fulfilled ("drop shipped" from home) until recently to control quality, then moved to a robot-run fulfillment center. Reviews are everything (~1,000 at 4.9/5). Optimize **online presence first** before chasing retail (retail = wholesale discount + demos + training). Margin target: **30-60%** (echoes the course); "take a hit on margin to earn the customer's trust."

## How Gretta Made $1M in a Single Day (The Fifth, first birthday)

The flagship case study of the whole course — a scarcity model compounded.

- **The Fifth Watches** — minimal, accessible watches (top style ~$180; ~10 SKUs total). The name came by accident (Gretta in an Uber on **Fifth Avenue**; co-founder Alex in Melbourne) and inspired the **"Fifth View"** concept (the popular downward/flat-lay shot), around which they built the Instagram account.
- **Audience before product.** Grew **~70K Instagram followers** pre-launch, converting **~8,800 into email subscribers**. Launched on the **5th of December** (Alex's birthday) — and **sold out / $100K on day one.**
- **Turn the sell-out accident into the UVP.** Worried customers would hate the sell-out, they reframed it: *"we're called The Fifth because we only sell on the 5th of each month, for 5 days, or until sold out."* That scarcity became the brand's **central UVP — exclusive by *time*, not price** (accessible price + limited availability → higher perceived value + scarcity-driven demand). Separately, a **manufacturing screw-up** (a vegetable-tan order came back the wrong peach/rose-gold color) became **50% of first-year sales** — another accident → defining feature.
- **The scarcity machine (the website ritual).** Every month they **changed the whole site UX**: 25 days a month there was no "add to cart" — the main CTA was email capture (enter your email *where* the product would be). At midnight on the 5th the site went **password-locked**; the password changed monthly and was emailed ~24 hours before launch, so members had to be on the waitlist to get in. Conversion rates ran **~90-95% in the first minutes** and **>50-60% for the first hour**. They engineered the "lining up overnight for a festival ticket" feeling — push notifications, calendar reminders, and **phone alarms** (a dopamine-reward ritual that killed buyer's remorse). Later they added **cart pre-loading**, and the first minute of the $1M day was ~a quarter of total sales.
- **Influencers at scale → the funnel.** ~200 influencer sendouts the month before the $1M day. **Influencer funnel**: awareness (influencers) → follow on social → **sign up to the waitlist** (the critical middle) → conversion. A 14-day Gleam **giveaway** (one watch/day — simple, attainable prizes work best) converted **~80K social followers into email subscribers** in two weeks. Tactics: lock fast-growing influencers into 3-month contracts at their *current* (smaller) rate; use **influencer clusters** (interrelated friend groups / reality-TV cohorts) for the **"everybody's doing it" / compounding effect** (overlap is the goal); find micro/nano lookalikes via Instagram's "similar to" rabbit hole using a blank list-building account; tier macro/mid/micro/nano (≤~4-5 macros/month). The **3 R's**: Reach, Relevancy (match the influencer's aesthetic to your feed or you cut your own funnel), Relationship (genuine engagement — "where'd you get that?" comments, video/Lives).
- **Email is the engine.** 40-60% of store sales came through email; ~50% repeat-customer rate attributed largely to email. Their **top email** was a one-line out-of-stock "back in stock, shop now" (~40% conversion of email traffic). The flagship **"24 hours to go" launch email** (reused every release): subject "They're coming — 24 hours to go," VIP-only first access, the store password in bold red, global launch times, and "set an alarm 5 minutes before sales begin." Sign-ups **direct to a specific product** (tappable bio grid → that watch's landing page) outperformed generic waitlist sign-ups.
- **The meta-lesson.** "What you're doing right now is **repeatable**." Gretta's biggest early fear was that overnight success would vanish overnight — instead she broke it into repeatable steps and ran the same playbook across **four multi-million-dollar e-commerce stores.**

## Mental Models
- **Be the Apple/Nike of your space** — sell a brand people want to be part of, not a commodity.
- **Turn accidents into UVPs** — The Fifth's sell-out and wrong color; OPV's two-tone; the playbook recurs.
- **Diversify so no single point can sink you** (Hirsch); **start scrappy and hands-on** (all five); **demand pulls the next product** (Alitura).
- **Influencer marketing is a compounding system at scale** — sum-total over months, foundation first, clusters for the "everybody's doing it" effect — not a few hopeful posts.
- **What works is repeatable** — systematize the win and run it again.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build a brand people want to belong to; differentiate a boring category on brand, not price.
2. Start scrappy, stay hands-on early, and diversify revenue/suppliers/people before you scale.
3. Treat manufacturing/color/sell-out accidents as candidate UVPs — reframe the negative into the positive.
4. The $1M day = audience-before-product + scarcity-by-time UVP + influencers-at-scale funneling into an email waitlist + a repeatable monthly ritual.
