# Chapter 7: Choosing Your Customers

## Core Idea
Startups don't starve, they drown — in options, leads, and ideas. A specific customer segment is your best friend: it cuts the noise so you can finally prove yourself right or wrong.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Segmentation**: "Before we can serve everyone, we have to serve someone." Big successes started narrow (Google → PhD students; eBay → Pez collectors; Evernote → moms sharing recipes).
- **Customer Slicing** — keep slicing a broad segment into ever-finer sub-sets until you have a **who-where pair**. Ask:
  - Within this group, who'd want it *most*? Would everyone buy/use it, or only some?
  - Why do they want it (problem/goal)? Who else shares that motivation?
  - What are they *already doing* to achieve the goal / survive the problem?
  - Where can we find the demographic groups and the workaround-doers?
- **Choose a starting segment** by who's most: (1) **Profitable**, (2) **Easy to reach**, (3) **Rewarding** to build a business around (and fun for you to be around).

## Key Concepts
- **Three problems of a fuzzy segment**: (1) overwhelmed by options, can't start; (2) not moving forward but can't prove yourself wrong; (3) wildly mixed feedback you can't parse.
- **"Babies or body builders?"** (superfood powder) and **"Big brands or mom & pop?"** (advertisers): serving everyone means a so-so product for many ≠ an incredible product for one.
- **The sneaky-broad segment**: "students" or "sales orgs with 25–250 reps" still hides unfathomable diversity. You weren't having 20 conversations with your customer — you were having one conversation each with 20 *different* customers.
- **Talking to the wrong people** — 3 ways: too-broad segment; missed segments (multi-sided markets, kids+parents, key partners); overlooked stakeholders in a complex B2B buying process. Talk to people *representative* of your customers, not the impressive-sounding ones.

## Mental Models
- The superfood-powder fix: go into small independent health-food stores (slice the segment), ask for shelf space (a commitment), return in a week. Cut through opinions by asking for a commitment.
- An industry expert can hand you a **taxonomy** of the market — a great starting point for where to slice.

## Anti-patterns
- Saying "yes" to every request because "those guys would love it" (and never being able to cut a feature).
- Talking to the most senior/important person instead of the representative user (suits vs. the teenagers who'd actually use it).

## Key Takeaways
1. **If you aren't finding consistent problems and goals, you don't yet have a specific enough customer segment.**
2. **Good customer segments are a who-where pair.** If you don't know where to find them, keep slicing.
3. You can broaden back out later — start specific, reachable, profitable, and personally rewarding.
