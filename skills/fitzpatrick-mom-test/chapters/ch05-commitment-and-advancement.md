# Chapter 5: Commitment and Advancement

## Core Idea
Once you reveal product, compliments come back. Cut through the false positives by asking for commitment — every meeting either succeeds (advances) or fails. Not asking is the only real failure.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Commitment & Advancement**:
  - **Commitment** — they give up something they *value*: time, reputation, or cash.
  - **Advancement** — they move to the next step of your real-world acquisition funnel.
  - They usually arrive hand-in-hand (e.g. intro to the boss = reputation commitment + advancement).
- **The currencies of conversation**: a compliment costs nothing, so it's worth nothing. Real currencies:
  - **Time**: next meeting with clear goals, feedback on wireframes, a non-trivial trial.
  - **Reputation**: intro to peers/team/decision-maker, public testimonial/case study.
  - **Cash**: letter of intent, pre-order, deposit.

## Key Concepts
- **Zombie leads**: "customers" who keep taking friendly meetings but never commit — you've been *friend-zoned*. Caused by being clingy and fearing rejection.
- **Earlyvangelists** (Steve Blank) — your crazy, precious first customers. In B2B they: have the problem, know they have it, have the budget, and have already cobbled together a makeshift solution. Watch for **deep emotion** ("THE WORST PART OF MY LIFE AND I'LL PAY YOU NOW").
- **Good meeting / bad meeting** examples: "Looks great, let me know when it launches" = compliment + stall (bad). "What are the next steps?" / "When can we start the trial?" / "Can I buy the prototype?" / "When can you talk to the rest of the team?" = good.

## Mental Models
- "There's no such thing as a meeting that just 'went well'." A meeting **succeeds or fails**.
- **Don't pitch blind**: hard pitching gives only binary yes/no feedback. Start with Mom-Test learning to get your bearings, *then* confirm by selling.
- Think in **currency**: if a trial is "too cheap to try," raise its cost (case-study commitment, team rollout, credit card on file).

## Anti-patterns
- **Not asking** for a commitment/next step — the real failure (rejection is fine; it's a clear negative data point).
- **Mistaking a compliment-stall for a pre-order** ("He said he'll buy it when it launches!").
- Treating "I would definitely buy that" as money in the bank.

## Key Takeaways
1. **If you don't know what happens next after a product or sales meeting, the meeting was pointless.**
2. **It's not a real lead until you've given them a concrete chance to reject you.**
3. **The more they're giving up, the more seriously you can take their kind words.**
4. **In early-stage sales, the real goal is learning. Revenue is just a side-effect.**
