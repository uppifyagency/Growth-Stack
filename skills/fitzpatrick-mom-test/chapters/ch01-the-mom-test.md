# Chapter 1: The Mom Test

## Core Idea
You shouldn't ask *anyone* whether your business is a good idea — it invites lies. The Mom Test is a set of rules for questions that even your mom can't lie to you about, because you never mention your idea.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Mom Test (3 rules)**:
  1. **Talk about their life instead of your idea.**
  2. **Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.**
  3. **Talk less and listen more.**
  - When to use: every early customer conversation.
  - How: ask about their world, dig into concrete past examples, shut up.
- **Good question / bad question** test: a question is bad if it asks for opinions, hypotheticals, or anything about the future or your idea.

## Key Concepts
- **A useful conversation** gives you **concrete facts about customers' lives and worldviews** that let you improve your business — not validation.
- **Failing example** (iPad cookbook): leading questions + pitching → mom lies to protect you. **Passing example**: ask what she last did, where she finds apps, where the cookbooks came from → gold dust about real behavior and segments.
- **They own the problem, you own the solution**: you aren't allowed to tell them their problem; they aren't allowed to tell you what to build.

## Mental Models
- Customer conversations are like a **delicate archaeological dig**: blunt questions (e.g. "do you think it's a good idea?") shatter the truth; flinching from depth finds nothing.
- "You find out if people care about what you're doing by **never mentioning it**."

## Anti-patterns
- **"Do you think it's a good idea?"** — only the market can tell; everything else is opinion.
- **"Would you buy a product that did X?"** / **"How much would you pay for X?"** — future hypotheticals; the number makes it feel truthy but it's worthless.
- Skipping lukewarm signals and barreling on to pitch more.

## Key Takeaways
1. It's your responsibility to find the truth via good questions — not theirs to volunteer it.
2. Mentioning your idea too soon is the most common (and biggest) mistake.
3. Just avoiding mentioning your idea automatically makes your questions better.
4. **Rule of thumb: Customer conversations are bad by default. It's your job to fix them.**
5. **Opinions are worthless. Anything involving the future is an over-optimistic lie.**
