# Patterns & Techniques — The Mom Test

## The Mom Test (the core technique)
**When to use**: every early customer conversation.
**How**: (1) talk about their life, not your idea; (2) ask for specifics in the past, not generics/opinions/future; (3) talk less, listen more.
**Trade-offs**: feels weird to validate by never mentioning the idea — but it's the easiest, biggest improvement you can make.

## Getting back on track (neutralizing bad data)
**When to use**: any time a conversation drifts to compliments, fluff, or ideas.
**How**:
- **Compliment** → deflect ("really sorry, I got excited — how are you handling this now?").
- **Fluff** → anchor ("When's the last time? Can you talk me through it? What else have you tried?").
- **Idea/feature request** → dig ("What would that let you do? How are you coping without it?").
- **Strong emotion** → dig ("Tell me more — I bet there's a story here").
**Trade-offs**: requires noticing the bad data in real time; the second note-taker helps.

## The good-question repertoire (passes the Mom Test)
"Why do you bother?" (motivation) · "What are the implications of that?" (does the problem matter / pricing signal) · "Talk me through the last time that happened." (show, don't tell) · "What else have you tried?" (alternatives + switching cost) · "How are you dealing with it now?" (price anchor) · "Where does the money come from?" (B2B budget/stakeholders) · "Who else should I talk to?" (end every chat) · "Is there anything else I should have asked?"

## Product risk vs. market risk triage
**When to use**: before deciding how much conversations can validate.
**How**: classify your biggest risk. Market risk (want/pay/enough?) → conversations validate it. Product risk (build/grow/retain?) → conversations only get you a starting point; you must build earlier. Watch for "if you could get me X, I'd pay" — that's product risk masquerading as validation.

## Commitment & advancement (cut through false positives)
**When to use**: once you reveal product / in sales-stage meetings.
**How**: every meeting must end with a real next step paid in a currency (time/reputation/cash). If a trial is too cheap to try, raise its cost (case study, team rollout, card on file). Don't pitch blind — Mom-Test-learn first, then sell.
**Trade-offs**: rejection is a *good* outcome (clear negative); only not-asking is failure.

## Framing a meeting — Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask
**When to use**: when a formal ask is unavoidable.
**How**: Vision (problem you're improving, no idea mentioned) → Framing (your stage; nothing to sell) → Weakness (the specific gap) → Pedestal (how they specifically can help) → Ask. Mnemonic: "Very Few Wizards Properly Ask."
**Trade-offs**: reorder freely (move weakness early if it reads too pitchy).

## Customer slicing → a who-where pair
**When to use**: feedback is all over the map / you don't know where to find people.
**How**: take a broad segment; ask who wants it most, why, what they're already doing, and where to find both the demographic and the workaround-doers. Repeat on the generic sub-groups. Pick a start by profitable / reachable / rewarding.
**Trade-offs**: you narrow now and broaden later; resist the urge to keep theorizing — slice just enough to go talk to someone.

## Running the process (before / during / after)
**Before**: choose a focused findable segment; decide the big 3 learning goals; decide ideal next steps/commitments; pick who to talk to; write best-guesses; do desk research first.
**During**: frame · keep it casual · ask Mom-Test questions · deflect/anchor/dig · take good notes (with signal symbols) · press for commitment.
**After**: review notes + key quotes with the team · transfer to permanent storage · update beliefs/plans · decide the next 3 questions.
**Trade-offs**: lightweight on purpose — it should *reduce* time spent. Sometimes just **hack it** and call them.

## The rules of thumb (the book's punchlines)
Customer conversations are bad by default — it's your job to fix them. · Opinions are worthless. · Anything involving the future is an over-optimistic lie. · Some problems don't actually matter. · If they haven't looked for a solution already, they won't buy yours. · Ideas should be understood, not obeyed. · The more you're talking, the worse you're doing. · You should be terrified of at least one question per conversation. · There's more reliable information in a "meh" than a "Wow!" · Start broad; zoom in only on a strong signal. · You always need a list of your 3 big questions. · If you don't know what happens next, the meeting was pointless. · It's not a real lead until you've given them a chance to reject you. · Good segments are a who-where pair. · Notes are useless if you don't look at them. · Go build your dang company already.
