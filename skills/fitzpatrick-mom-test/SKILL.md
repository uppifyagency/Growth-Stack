## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Mom Test (the central framework)
A set of rules for crafting questions that **even your mom can't lie to you about** — because you never mention your idea. Three rules:
1. **Talk about their life instead of your idea.**
2. **Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.**
3. **Talk less and listen more.**

> **It's not the customer's job to show us the truth. It's ours to find it — by asking good questions.** Customer conversations are bad *by default*; it's your job to fix them. The big mistake is almost always mentioning your idea **too soon**.

### The core trap
**Bad data is worse than no data.** It gives false positives that make you over-invest cash, time, and team. "Doing it wrong is worse than doing nothing at all" — collecting false positives is "like convincing a drunk he's sober."

### The 3 types of bad data (and how to get back on track)
1. **Compliments** → **Deflect** them. "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, entirely worthless." Opinions are worthless (even VCs are wrong more than right).
2. **Fluff** (generics: "I usually/always/never"; future promises: "I would/will"; hypotheticals: "I might/could") → **Anchor** it back to specifics in the past. The world's deadliest fluff: **"I would definitely buy that."**
3. **Ideas / feature requests** → **Dig beneath** them to the motivation. "Ideas and feature requests should be understood, but not obeyed." (MTV "branded reports" story.)
- Also: **stop seeking approval** (fishing for compliments + the **Pathos Problem** — exposing your ego makes people protect your feelings), **cut off pitches** ("Anyone will say your idea is great if you're annoying enough about it"), and **talk less** ("The more you're talking, the worse you're doing").

### Asking important questions
Ask questions that could **destroy your currently imagined business**. "You should be terrified of at least one of the questions you're asking in every conversation." And **love bad news** — "There's more reliable information in a 'meh' than a 'Wow!'"
- **Avoid the premature zoom**: start broad; don't zoom in on a problem until you have a strong signal it's a must-solve. People have many problems they won't pay to fix.
- **Product risk vs. Market risk** — know which you have:
  - **Product risk**: Can I build it? Can I grow it? Will they keep using it?
  - **Market risk**: Do they want it? Will they pay? Are there enough of them?
  Conversations validate *market* risk; heavy *product* risk means you must start building earlier with less certainty.
- **Prepare your list of 3** big questions before each conversation type.

### Commitment & Advancement
Once you reveal product, cut through false positives by asking for commitment.
- **Commitment** — they give up something they value: **time, reputation, or cash**.
- **Advancement** — they move to the next step of your real-world funnel.
- "There's no such thing as a meeting that just 'went well'." A meeting **succeeds or fails**. The real failure is **not asking** for the next step → **zombie leads** (friend-zoned).
- **Earlyvangelists** (your crazy first customers): have the problem, know it, have the budget, and have already cobbled together a makeshift solution. "In early-stage sales, the real goal is learning. Revenue is just a side-effect."

### Finding conversations & framing the meeting
- **Keep it casual** — a chat beats a formal meeting; if it feels like they're doing you a favour, it's too formal.
- **Bringing them to you** > going to them: organize meetups, teach, blog, get clever, build warm intros. The goal of cold calls is to *stop having them*.
- **Framing a meeting** — **Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask** (mnemonic: "Very Few Wizards Properly Ask [for help]"). Don't mention your idea.
- **The advisory flip**: go looking for *advisors*, not customers — it removes neediness and puts you in control.

### Choosing your customers (segmentation)
"Before we can serve everyone, we have to serve someone." **Good customer segments are a who-where pair** — if you don't know where to find them, keep **customer slicing** into smaller pieces. "If you aren't finding consistent problems and goals, you don't yet have a specific enough customer segment."

### Running the process
Avoid the **learning bottleneck** (one person hoards the learning → de-facto dictator). Three parts: **prep** (know your 3 questions; "What do we want to learn from these guys?"), **converse** (Mom Test + casual + commitment), **review** (with the team, with notes + exact quotes). **Notes are useless if you don't look at them.** And don't over-process: sometimes you just **hack it** (pick up the phone). This stuff is fast — go build your company.

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the book only — *how to talk to customers and learn from them*, one big part of Customer Development (Steve Blank's wider process is out of scope). It's "how," not "why." For executing in your context, combine with your product, your segment, and real conversations. Deciding *what to build* is your job: they own the problem, you own the solution.
