# Chapter 6: Monetise Your Empire

## Core Idea
YouTube AdSense is the *worst* way to make money on YouTube — "the way you really accelerate your monetization is by taking it off of YouTube and building your own stuff." Build a large audience on public platforms, funnel it to platforms you own (email list above all), then layer **many** diversified revenue pillars on top so no single product can sink you. The biggest money is in **personal products**, earned by years of free value (jab) before any ask (right hook).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 5 Ways to Monetise (the monetisation stack)** — ranked roughly worst→best money: (1) **AdSense / CPMs**, (2) **Affiliate links**, (3) **Sponsors**, (4) **Personal products**, plus the underlying audience→owned-platform→paid funnel. Use when planning revenue; never rely on only one. "The real interesting place is when you're able to sell your own personal products."
- **CPM vs RPM** — CPM = cost per *mille* (per 1,000 ad impressions, what the advertiser pays); **RPM = revenue per mille** (what *you* keep per 1,000 views after YouTube's ~45-50% cut). Use RPM, not CPM, to model income: avg views ÷ 1,000 × your RPM. Standard RPM ≈ $1-3; finance/business RPM ≈ $20-40 ("10x more revenue for the same views").
- **The 3 Factors Affecting Ad Revenue** — **Niche · Watch Time · Geography**. Use to predict/raise AdSense: pick a high-CPM niche (finance, business, money attract credit-card/crypto/course advertisers); longer videos earn more (watch time maps onto revenue far better than views — anything >20 min beat 10-20 min beat <10 min on his channel); US/UK viewers monetise far better than India (lower RPM + lower avg view duration), so even *upload time* can be tuned toward the profitable audience.
- **Jab, Jab, Jab, Right Hook** (Gary Vaynerchuk) — jabs = free value/content; the right hook = the ask (buy, subscribe, donate). Use when selling: give *far* more free content than you ever ask for. Ali sold nothing for 3.5 years of jabbing; his first right-hook (PTYA) expected 5-10 signups and got ~350.
- **The "Create an Affiliate Program" cold-email play** — when a product you genuinely use has *no* public affiliate program, email the founder, position yourself as the buyer, pitch the brand-creator fit, and ask them to *create* a program (often via a Shopify app like vwa.la). "If you don't ask it's a default no." Then later ask to **bump the rate** (Paperlike 10%→20%; iQUNIX 5%→15%) — "that one email has probably been the highest-ROI email I've ever sent."

## Key Concepts
- **AdSense eligibility (YouTube Partner Program)** — needs 1,000 subscribers + 4,000 watch hours in the last 12 months; turn ads on the instant you qualify (YouTube now shows ads on non-monetised channels anyway, so withholding them just forfeits revenue).
- **Niche premium** — advertiser spend, not view count, sets RPM; finance/business/money is the highest-paying niche ("if I started YouTube today it'd be personal finance / investing / crypto").
- **Affiliate links** — earn a % of sales you refer. Amazon Associates = biggest, easiest, ~1-3% commission. Skillshare = unusually generous: ~$7-10 per *free-trial* signup (viewer pays nothing). Find programs by Googling "[product] affiliate program."
- **Upload defaults** — set your channel's default video description so every upload auto-includes your affiliate links; disclose ("some links are affiliate links I get a kickback from").
- **Brand-creator fit** — disproportionate affiliate returns come when the product maps onto your content (Paperlike screen protector ↔ iPad note-taking videos → ~$50k/yr from a couple of emails + a few videos).
- **Sponsors** — standard rate ≈ $15 per 1,000 views (based on last-10-video average). High-friction (advance shooting, approvals, contracts) — not worth it until ~20k subs / 100 videos / a genuinely interesting sum.
- **Personal products** — the real money: online courses, books, digital downloads, Patreon, custom membership, consulting, physical products.
- **Skillshare economics** — pays *per minute of premium watch time* (like Spotify royalties), ~$2-4 per student; non-exclusive (you keep IP, just can't host the full class free elsewhere). More classes → more money; one Final Cut class still earns $3-5k/mo passively years later.
- **Email list = the most important platform you own** — public platforms can change rules or cancel you; the owned audience is your hedge.

## Mental Models
- **Watch time maps onto revenue more than views do** — think of a long, high-retention video as worth more than a higher-view short one; "there's nothing wrong with making really long videos."
- **A large audience + diverse products = a hedge against future uncertainty** — treat over-reliance on any one revenue source (AdSense, then sponsors, then Skillshare, then PTYA) as a "house of cards"; the cure is always *the next pillar*.
- **Be the buyer, not the seller, in brand emails** — reply terse and high-status ("wireless or wired?"; "I'm afraid I can't justify spending $300"); flip a free-product pitch into a paid deal or an affiliate program.
- **Target people with money (or who make/save money from what you sell)** — Skillshare suits a broke/student audience; self-hosted $150-500 courses suit an audience with disposable income.
- **High-touch vs low-touch income** — coaching/consulting is *active* and works with a tiny audience (20 × $2k = $40k/yr); courses/Skillshare are *passive* and need a large audience to be worth it.

## Anti-patterns
- Expecting monetisation early — it took Ali 6 months / 52 videos to hit 1k subs; the average channel takes ~152 videos to its first 1,000 subscribers. Complaining at 20-30 videos = expectations out of whack with the data.
- Relying on a single revenue stream — feels safe, is fragile; diversify before you're forced to.
- Chasing sponsors too early — high-friction, low-relative-money below ~20k subs.
- Treating AdSense as the goal — it's the floor, not the ceiling; the off-YouTube products are where it compounds.
- Going in for the right hook without enough jabs — selling to an audience you haven't given free value to.
- Self-hosting a paid course for a broke audience (or putting a broke-audience product behind a $150 paywall) — match the price model to who's actually watching.
- Defaulting to "no ask" — not asking for the deal, the rate bump, or the new affiliate program guarantees zero.

## Key Takeaways
1. Money ladder: AdSense < affiliates < sponsors < your own products — and the email list underpins all of it.
2. Model income on **RPM**, then move the levers you control: niche, watch time (go long), geography (upload-time targeting).
3. Affiliate links are near-free money once set via upload defaults; cold-email founders to create or bump a program — brand-creator fit makes it disproportionate.
4. Skip sponsors until ~20k subs / 100 videos; they're a faff with low relative pay.
5. Jab for years before the right hook — front-loaded free value is what makes the eventual sale land huge.
6. Skillshare = passive Spotify-style royalties for a no-money audience; self-hosted courses, cohort courses, books, downloads, Patreon, membership, and consulting are how you actually get paid.
7. Diversify relentlessly — a large audience + many revenue pillars is your insurance against the next platform change or cancellation.
