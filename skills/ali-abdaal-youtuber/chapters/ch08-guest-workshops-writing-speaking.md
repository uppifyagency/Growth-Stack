# Chapter 8: Guest Workshops II — Writing, Storytelling & Speaking

## Core Idea
Your video is only as good as the writing, story, and delivery underneath it. Write first to think clearly, structure every video around a transformation told in scenes, and treat speaking as a trainable *skill of access* (not talent) — confidence is something you put on, not wait for.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Tell, Title, Tension** (David Perell — the scripted intro trifecta) — In the first ~50 words / under 20 seconds, do three things: **tell** the viewer what they'll get, mention the **title**, and create **tension** (open a question that hangs). Use when scripting any video intro to fix the retention drop-off in the first 30 seconds. How: reference the title in the first two sentences, stack stats to "raise the magnitude," and end the intro on a question disguised as a statement. "Get to the point — not a single word wasted."
- **C.L.E.A.R.** (David Perell — clear sentences) — **C**reate a rhythm (vary sentence length; "don't just write words, write music"); **L**ink your sentences (each connects to the main idea, follows the previous, flows into the next — add transition "chains" like *however / so*); **E**liminate confusion (avoid the "Norman door" — handles say pull, sign says push); **A**dd colorful details (be specific: "Lynda drove her Mercedes to the shop for new brake pads," not "the woman took her car in for repairs"); **R**emove unnecessary words (delete "consulting words" — synergy, disruptive, empower). Use when editing a script.
- **Why You Should Write** (David Perell) — Writing is thinking. Externalizing ideas on the page sharpens them (as printing-press literacy sharpened Industrial-Revolution thinking) → clearer thinking → better videos and better speaking. Use to justify scripting before filming.
- **Homework for Life** (Matthew Dicks) — A daily 5-minute practice: at day's end, write one line answering "what was the most *story-worthy* moment today?" (the moment you'd tell if your family were kidnapped and only a story would free them). Use to never run out of stories; it builds a "lens" so you start *seeing* the stories already in your life.
- **The Five-Second Moment** (Matthew Dicks) — Every story is about **transformation or realization** — "I used to be one kind of person, then something happened, now I'm different." That change happens in a single ~5-second instant (keys placed in his hand → "I'm a selfish jerk"). Use as the *ending* of your story; find the beginning by stating its **opposite**, so beginning and end are "in conversation."
- **Cinema of Storytelling** (Matthew Dicks) — Location is key: tell the story in **scenes** (movies always show you where you are). Use to make a story vivid and memorable; sketch the locations to remember the sequence (his stories are "almost always seven scenes").
- **Performative Speaking** (Robbie Crabtree) — Speaking = *performance art* ("performative") + *words that bring about change* ("performativity"). Use when delivering to camera: connect emotionally, treat words as art that changes a viewer's thinking.
- **The Four Delivery Tactics** (Robbie Crabtree) — (1) **Slow down** (kills filler words — your brain keeps up); (2) **The camera is a person** (picture one specific avatar → warmth in your voice); (3) **Less is more** (10-word answer: "if you have two minutes, deliver it in one"; "build the wall"); (4) **Open hands / anchor by location** (open hands = safety in human DNA; stand in fixed spots to anchor ideas). Plus **silence/pauses** as the most underused tool — opens a loop, makes audiences lean in, and covers a blank.
- **Rapid Fire Analogies · Conductor · The Snow Globe** (Ultraspeaking — Tristan & Michael) — Three games to train delivery: **Rapid Fire Analogies** (finish "X is like Y because…" before the next random word appears → trains *access* + staying in character); **Conductor** (match your energy to a changing number 1–10 → trains range); **The Snow Globe** (inject breaths/pauses so the "snow" of your idea settles and stays visible). Use to practice before pressing record.

## Key Concepts
- **Stay in character / don't break character** — The single rule: never signal nervousness ("I guess I messed up," fidgeting, apologizing). Most people can't tell you're nervous *unless you tell them*.
- **Game of access, not skill** — You've spoken since age ~13 months; you're already a great speaker — confidence is *accessed*, not learned from scratch.
- **Reframe nerves as energy/excitement** — Nervousness and excitement are the same bodily signal; "I'm excited" raises energy and self-belief. (Crabtree + Ultraspeaking agree.)
- **Confidence = competence (to the audience)** — Audiences judge competence by confidence, even though it's not true; so you must *own it*.
- **Mindset matches skill set = flow** — Diving in fully ("I'm just going to be in it") beats sitting back trying to control every word.
- **Pausing is the most powerful tool in speaking** — Access to the breath unlocks everything; sit in silence rather than fill it with "um."
- **Benevolent coercion** (Dicks) — Get people inside your story and they'll *feel* with you, which earns the right to teach them a lesson.
- **Beginnings & endings are the most valuable territory** (Dicks) — Begin with **forward movement** (close to the end); the beginning is a promise, the ending is the payoff/transformation.
- **Imposter syndrome destroys speakers** (Crabtree) — You have a voice, a vision, a story worth sharing; "practice a different set of messages."
- **Tactics = musicality** (Crabtree) — Rhythm, pacing, tone make words "musical," which the creative brain stores (like song lyrics). Strategy = the big idea; tactics = how you deliver it.

## Mental Models
- **Think of speaking as a snow globe** — every word shakes it; if you never pause, the snow never settles and the audience loses the idea. Breathe to let it settle.
- **Use the "opposite" trick to frame a story** — once you have the five-second realization (the end), the beginning is simply its opposite; the two in conversation create a complete arc.
- **Treat confidence as a costume you put on** — energy, posture, and "believe you're confident" change the output even while you still *feel* nervous inside.
- **Write for a translator** — simpler, clearer, fewer words; if it's easy to translate, it's clear.

## Anti-patterns
- Filming unscripted intros — viewers drop in the first 30 seconds; a big setup wastes the only words that matter.
- Telling the audience you're nervous (or fidgeting/apologizing) — that's the *only* way they find out.
- Filling pauses with "um / uh / like" — you're getting "over your skis"; silence reads as intentional and powerful instead.
- "Consulting words" (synergy, disrupt, empower) and complicated academic sentences — nobody talks like that; delete them.
- Reporting events ("here's what happened yesterday") instead of telling a moment of change — there's no transformation, so no story.
- Over-preparing and trying to control every word — it raises nervousness; you want flow, not a memorized script you can break.
- Speaking too fast — almost everyone does; you'll feel "too slow" exactly when you've hit the right pace.

## Key Takeaways
1. Script your intro around **Tell, Title, Tension** and open a question that hangs — that's where retention is won or lost.
2. Edit every sentence with **C.L.E.A.R.**: rhythm, links, no confusion, vivid specifics, no filler. Write music, not words.
3. Run **Homework for Life** daily so you always have stories; build each story around a **five-second moment** of transformation, framed by its opposite, told in **scenes**.
4. Reframe nerves as energy, **stay in character**, and never tell anyone you're nervous — confidence is accessed, not earned.
5. Master the four tactics — **slow down, camera-is-a-person, less-is-more, open hands** — and use **silence** as your strongest move.
6. Practice with games (**Rapid Fire Analogies, Conductor, Snow Globe**) before recording — speaking is a skill: learned, practiced, mastered.
